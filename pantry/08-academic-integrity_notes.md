## A. Conceptual foundations

### Concept 1 — Ethical beliefs predict AI use, not policy awareness

The MDPI 2025 study of 401 students at major U.S. universities found that students' *ethical beliefs* about AI use predicted their actual AI use behavior; *policy awareness* did not. Most students knew their institution's policy. They acted on what they believed about whether AI use was wrong, not what they had been told was prohibited.

The implication for policy design: a policy that only states what is prohibited, without articulating *why*, is acting on the wrong variable. The variable is student belief. The lever is shared moral reasoning about what learning is for and what authorship means.

The pantry research file flags this finding as load-bearing. The chapter's policy framework should be organized around making the *reasoning* visible to students, not just the *rules*.

**Common misconception:** "Students cheat because they don't know the rules." False. Students who chose to use AI inappropriately mostly knew the rule. They believed using AI in that context was not really wrong, or that it was a reasonable shortcut, or that the assignment was inauthentic anyway.

**Source(s):** MDPI 2025 study (pantry/notebooklm_education_research.md, Academic Integrity section); supporting work in academic integrity literature (McCabe, Treviño & Butterfield 2001 onward on the role of moral climate in cheating behavior).

### Concept 2 — The FSU red flags as behavioral markers

The Florida State University guidance identifies four behavioral markers indicating a student's relationship with AI has crossed a line:

1. The student cannot explain the intellectual steps to a conclusion without referencing the AI
2. The student uses the tool to generate complete homework responses rather than to understand materials
3. The student relies entirely on summaries and avoids engaging with original texts
4. The student experiences heightened anxiety when forced to complete exams without digital assistance

These are *observable*, *behavioral*, and *operational* — meaning a teacher can use them as a diagnostic without needing to read the student's mind or run a polygraph. They are framed as flags for *conversation*, not for punishment — the right response to a flagged behavior is a one-on-one conversation about the student's learning, not an honor-code charge.

**Source(s):** FSU faculty AI guidance (pantry research file references this; verify current URL).

---

### Concept 3 — Source-grounding reduces one category of integrity violation but not all

NotebookLM's source-grounding reduces *fabrication* — the student cannot present made-up facts as cited. Every claim has a source link.

It does not reduce *substitution* — the student can still submit AI-synthesized analysis as their own. Citation discipline is orthogonal to authorship.

The chapter must be precise about which integrity questions NotebookLM helps with and which it doesn't. Conflating these produces policies that are either too lax (treating source-grounding as if it solved integrity entirely) or too strict (banning the tool that solves one problem because it doesn't solve all of them).

**Source(s):** pantry research file "False Confidence from Grounded AI" section.

---

### Concept 4 — AI use disclosure as policy mechanism

A practical policy mechanism: students must disclose AI use as part of submitting work. The disclosure includes:
- Whether NotebookLM was used at all
- What sources were uploaded
- What outputs were generated
- What the student accepted, rejected, or revised

This framing has multiple effects:
- It makes the use *visible* (and therefore discussable)
- It requires the student to *reflect on* their use (which is itself a metacognitive exercise)
- It distinguishes the student who used NotebookLM to understand material (low risk) from the student who used it to produce the deliverable (high risk)

Several universities have moved toward this framework in 2024-2025. The chapter should treat it as a current default option rather than a novel idea.

**Source(s):** pantry research file "Assessment Redesign" section; multiple university AI guidance documents (UCLA, Penn, Harvard, others — verify current versions).

## B. Domain examples and cases

### Case 1 — The student who can't explain (FSU red flag #1)

A student submits an excellent essay on the Treaty of Versailles. The teacher asks them in office hours: "What's the most important sentence in your essay?" The student cannot point to one. They cannot reconstruct the argument. They cannot defend why they made the particular interpretive choice they made.

This is the case where the surface artifact looks fine but the cognitive work that should have produced it did not happen. The integrity question is not "did they use AI" (probably yes, and that may have been permitted); it is "did they learn what the assignment was designed to teach" (no). The conversation is about learning, not about violation.

### Case 2 — The disclosure that reveals the right pattern

A student submits with the disclosure: *"I uploaded the assigned readings to NotebookLM, generated a study guide and 20 flashcards, took the flashcards three times until I got 100%, then drafted the essay without the tool open. I used NotebookLM once during revision to check whether a claim I'd made was actually supported by source 3."*

This disclosure shows productive use. The student used NotebookLM as a study aid (Tier 1 work — pattern matching, retrieval practice) and did the synthesis (Tier 4-7 work — judgment, argument, accountability) themselves. The disclosure makes this visible to the teacher, who could otherwise only see the final essay and have no way to know.

### Failure case — Ban without conversation

An institution bans student NotebookLM use and devotes its policy energy to detection and enforcement. The MDPI finding suggests students who already believed AI use was fine will continue. Students who were uncertain now do not get the help they need to reason about it. The shared moral climate around authorship and learning, which is the actual lever, is not built. Result: enforcement workload up, integrity outcomes flat.

## D. Current state of the field

**Settled:**
- Students' ethical beliefs about AI use are stronger predictors of behavior than policy awareness (MDPI 2025)
- AI use disclosure as a policy mechanism is now standard practice at major U.S. universities
- AI detection tools are unreliable at the level needed for enforcement decisions (Liang et al. 2023)

**Contested or emerging:**
- Whether "honor code"-style approaches (build climate of integrity) outperform "compliance"-style approaches (detect and punish) in AI-augmented contexts
- Whether policies should be uniform across courses or course-specific
- Whether the disclosure requirement creates incentive to misrepresent use ("disclosure laundering")

## E. Teaching considerations

**Where readers get stuck:**
- They want a policy template they can adopt without thinking. The chapter resists this; the policy has to be locally reasoned to be locally defensible.
- They believe a stricter policy is a safer policy. The MDPI finding is that policy strictness has little effect; what matters is the conversation the policy enables.
- They conflate fabrication and substitution. The chapter must keep these distinct.

**Analogies that work:**
- The calculator policy in math classes (1990s onward): the question was never "can students use them"; it was "what does the assessment ask them to do that the calculator can't?" Same logic.
- The peer-review norm in scholarship: scholars use each other's work; the norm is disclosure (citation), not prohibition.

**Exercises:**
- Create level: Draft a one-paragraph AI use policy for one of your courses. Share it with a colleague who is skeptical of AI tools. Identify the question they ask that the policy fails to address.
- Evaluate level: Given three student submissions (one with full disclosure of NotebookLM use; one with no disclosure but written-by-AI signals; one with no disclosure and human writing patterns), determine which (if any) merits an integrity conversation and why.

## G. Gaps and flags

- **FLAG:** AI policy is contested intellectual territory. The chapter takes a position (disclosure-plus-context over prohibition-plus-detection). Author should explicitly name the dissent and provide the strongest version of the counter-argument.
- **GAP:** No published longitudinal study yet of how disclosure-based policies affect integrity outcomes over multiple academic years. The chapter argues from short-term evidence and theoretical considerations.
- **FLAG:** District- and institution-specific legal frameworks (Title IX, due process for honor-code violations) constrain policy choices. The chapter should briefly note that local legal review is required for any policy.
